# You don’t need everyone to believe before they begin

A delivery manager proposed a shared onboarding checklist to stop the inconsistent experience. The team leads raised concerns about extra admin work and differences between projects.

The manager removed steps that duplicated existing records and clarified which parts each project could adapt. But some leads remained unconvinced that the checklist would help. He held another round of sanity checks, asked for more feedback, and kept waiting for everyone to get behind the idea.

The next hire arrived. The team used the same scattered onboarding process while the proposed checklist sat unused. They had more opinions about whether it would work, but zero experience with it.

Asking for feedback has a purpose: expose problems, challenge assumptions, and improve the decision. It doesn’t need to end with everyone wanting the same thing. Some questions need an answer before you proceed. Others, including whether the change delivers the expected benefit, need a test.

That doesn’t mean barking orders and dismissing concerns. Give them room to challenge the approach and address the points raised. Then make the decision, be clear about the execution you expect, and specify what results makes you continue vs which result makes you stop and go back to the drawing board.

People don’t have to pretend they agree. They do have to follow through with the agreed work for the test to mean anything. If everyone skips the parts they doubt, you won’t learn whether the idea works.

The same standard applies to you. Decide what would count as success before the results arrive, and judge the idea against that. If it works, people have evidence to support it. If it fails, swallow your ego, admit you were wrong, and move on to the next idea.
