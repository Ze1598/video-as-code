# Cooperation without consequences rewards selfishness

Two delivery teams agreed to lend each other people when deadlines got tight. When one team needed help finishing a release, the other lent a developer.

When the lending team needed support, the other manager refused to move anyone. He wanted to keep his team’s plans intact.

If you judge cooperation by what it costs today, refusing can look like the better choice. You keep your people, protect your schedule, and let someone else deal with their problem. But you will work with those people again. At some point, you will be the one who needs their time, knowledge, or help.

That ongoing relationship gives cooperation its value. Refusing every request can save you capacity in the short term and leave you without help when your own plans fall apart.

That consequence has to exist for trusted cooperation. If someone receives the same support whether they contribute or refuse, they can keep the benefit without paying the cost. Meanwhile, those who cooperate learn that helping earns them more demands, with no reason to expect help in return. You make withdrawal the better deal for both sides.

Building a cooperative team means making continued cooperation worth choosing. Start by helping, and return the help you receive. Give people reason to expect that their contribution matters beyond the task in front of them.

A genuine inability to help isn’t the same as a pattern of taking without contributing. Address that pattern when it appears. Make clear that people who keep refusing to make room for others cannot expect others to keep making room for them. Stop granting the same access to support, and let a return to cooperation rebuild it.
