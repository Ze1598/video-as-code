# Stop guessing who belongs in the meeting

The account manager invited the architect and project manager to the internal kickoff for a new project. They went through the requirements, discussed the architecture, and agreed that the two would share the details with the delivery team.

The questions started when they briefed the developers. Parts of the architecture depended on capabilities the systems didn’t support. Other requirements involved work not accounted for in the timeline. The people responsible for implementation saw problems that the planning discussion missed.

The architect and project manager took those concerns back to the account manager. Questions that belonged in the kickoff became another round of discussions, and around and around they went between groups.

Keeping the original invite list small split the conversation across meetings.

You can’t decide who belongs in a meeting by looking at the management hierarchy. The people responsible for a team don’t replace everyone whose work is being discussed. Passing on a summary gives those people information, but removes their chance to question assumptions, explain constraints, and hear the answers from the people who have them.

The opposite mistake is to invite everyone with a connection to the topic. Half the room spends an hour listening to something that should have been an email summary for them. Being on the same project doesn’t mean every discussion needs the entire team.

Before sending the invitation, ask what each person needs from the discussion. Will it affect their work in a way they need to understand, question, or help decide? Include them. Are they adjacent to the topic and only need to know what was agreed? Send them the outcome.

Bring the people who need to be involved. Don’t make everyone else attend to stay informed.
